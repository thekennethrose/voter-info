How voting works

Two kinds of voting shape federal law: yours, in elections, and your members of Congress's, on bills and nominations. Here is each in plain terms, with links to official sources for the full picture.

How you vote

Who represents you

Every state has two senators and at least one representative: 100 senators and 435 representatives in all. Senators serve six-year terms, staggered so about a third of the Senate is up for election every two years. Representatives serve two-year terms, so the whole House is up every two years.

House seats are divided among the states by population after each census, and each representative is elected by one district. Washington, D.C., Puerto Rico and the territories elect delegates who can speak and serve on committees but can't vote on final passage of bills.

Find yours: house.gov, Find your representative, or search this site's directory.

When federal elections happen

Federal general elections are held on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November in even-numbered years. Presidential elections come every four years; the elections in between are called midterms. Before the general election, parties choose their nominees through primaries or caucuses, with rules and dates set by each state and party.

More: usa.gov, Congressional, state and local elections.

Registering and casting a ballot

States and local governments run elections, so registration deadlines, ID requirements, early voting and voting by mail all differ by state. Check your own state's rules well before an election.

More: usa.gov, Register to vote; usa.gov, How, when and where to vote; U.S. Election Assistance Commission, Voters.

How the President is elected

The President is chosen by the Electoral College. Each state gets as many electors as it has senators and representatives combined, and D.C. gets three, for 538 in all; a candidate needs 270 to win. In most states the winner of the state's popular vote gets all of its electors. Maine and Nebraska award some electors by congressional district.

More: National Archives, About the Electoral College; usa.gov, How the President is elected.

How Congress votes

How a bill becomes law

  1. A member introduces a bill in the House or the Senate.
  2. It goes to a committee, which can hold hearings, change it, approve it or set it aside. Most bills never leave committee.
  3. If it comes out of committee, the full chamber debates it and votes, often on amendments first.
  4. If it passes, the other chamber goes through the same steps.
  5. Both chambers must pass identical text. They settle differences by amending each other's version or through a conference committee.
  6. The President signs it into law or vetoes it. A bill the President neither signs nor vetoes within ten days (Sundays excepted) becomes law anyway, unless Congress has adjourned; then it's a pocket veto.
  7. Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote in each chamber.

More: usa.gov, How laws are made; house.gov, The legislative process.

How a vote is taken

Many decisions are made by voice vote, where members call out yea or nay together, or by unanimous consent, where something passes unless anyone objects. Neither records how individual members voted.

A roll call vote records every member's position by name. In the Senate, a roll call is held when one-fifth of a quorum asks for one; the House votes electronically. Only roll call votes appear on this site.

More: senate.gov, Voting; house.gov, House floor.

How many votes it takes

ThresholdUsed for
Simple majority of those votingMost votes in both chambers, including passing bills. Also Senate cloture on nominations, since 2013 (2017 for Supreme Court nominees).
Three-fifths of all senators (60)Senate cloture on most legislation, and waiving most budget rules.
Two-thirds of those votingOverriding a veto (in each chamber), passing a House bill under suspension of the rules, approving a treaty in the Senate, and proposing a constitutional amendment.

When the Senate ties, the Vice President may cast the deciding vote. In the House, a tie fails.

More: senate.gov, Filibuster and cloture.

Why one bill can have many votes

A bill can get a dozen or more roll call votes before it passes or fails: on whether to start debate (a motion to proceed), whether to end debate (cloture), the House rule for debating it, amendments, motions to recommit or table, and final passage.

These votes decide different questions. A vote on cloture or on a motion to proceed is about whether and when the chamber takes something up, not a direct vote on the bill's contents. On each member's page, the vote title says what was being decided, and the glossary explains each kind of vote.

Votes only the Senate takes

The President nominates cabinet secretaries, federal judges and other officials, and the Senate decides whether to confirm them, a power called advice and consent. The Senate also approves treaties. Representatives don't vote on either, so only senators' pages have a Nominations tab.

More: senate.gov, Nominations.
